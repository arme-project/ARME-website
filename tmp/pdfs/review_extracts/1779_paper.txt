Multimodal Guidance under IK-Mediated Embodiment: Topological Limits
and Gender-Moderated Effects
Category: Research
Figure 1: IK-mediated multimodal guidance in sparse-tracking VR: visual puppet linkages with optional pitch auditory and haptic

ABSTRACT
Combining, otherwise known as ‘stacking’, auditory and haptic
cues with visual guidance is a common strategy for improving on-
line error correction in VR training. However, under sparse 6-
DoF tracking with inverse kinematics (IK), multimodal benefits
may be limited by solver-induced kinematic ambiguity and may
not generalize across users. We investigated this question in a VR
motor-imitation study (N=104) with four feedback conditions:
visual only (V), visual+audio (V A), visual+haptic (VH), and vi-
sual+audio+haptic (V AH). Results show that multimodal efficacy
is constrained by IK topology and learning-stage trade-offs. Along
the kinematic chain, feedback effects were absent at solver-driven
joints (elbows), exhibited trend-level divergence at sensor-driven
joints (wrists), and were strongest at hierarchy-driven distal seg-
ments (fingers). Visuo-haptic guidance (VH) accelerated online
adaptation relative to the visual baseline, but this benefit did not
carry over to the full stack (V AH). Instead, V AH was associated
with lower offline cued recall, indicating a trade-off between exe-
cution support and short-term retention. We also observed a distal
Condition×Gender interaction—haptic inclusion improved perfor-
mance in the male group but degraded performance in the female
group under the same mapping, consistent with a confirmation-
versus-interference pattern. Based on these findings, we derive
three design guidelines for IK-mediated VR training—Topology-
Aware Masking, Interference Detection, and Scaffold-and-Wean
Scheduling.
Index Terms:Virtual reality, multimodal feedback, inverse kine-
matics, motor learning, gender differences.
1 INTRODUCTION
A central challenge in consumer VR training is providing action-
able error information under sparse 6-DoF tracking, typically lim-
ited to the head and hands. Although visual guidance (e.g. trajec-
tory overlays) has shown promise [9], its fidelity is fundamentally
constrained by inverse kinematics (IK) reconstruction. IK intro-
duces a solver-dependent “black box”—including non-unique joint
solutions and temporal smoothing—that can obscure fine-grained
motor deviations [14]. In this case, the “solver” refers to the al-
gorithm that reconstructs untracked joint poses from sparse tracked
inputs. As a result, users may struggle to accurately perceive and
correct motion errors online (i.e. as the training is occuring). A
common mitigation strategy ismodality stacking, in which visual
guidance is augmented with auditory sonification and/or vibrotac-
tile cues. Under classical multisensory integration, combining inde-
pendent sensory estimates should reduce perceptual variance [12].
However, empirical findings in VR remain mixed. For example,
some studies showed that auxiliary cues can also compete for at-
tention or introduce sensory conflict [19, 41].
These inconsistencies suggest that multimodal benefits may de-
pend jointly on system-level estimation fidelity and user hetero-
geneity. Classical integration theories assume that modalities
provide partially independent observations of a common latent
state, enabling reliability-weighted variance reduction [39]. In IK-
mediated VR, however, multiple feedback channels are often de-
rived from the same solver-mediated error signal, which may con-
tain both temporal fluctuations (dynamic noise) and systematic off-
sets (spatial bias). In this setting, adding channels does not neces-
sarily add independent information.
At the same time, prior psychophysical work reports group-
level variability in sensorimotor strategies, including differences
in reference-frame preference and sensory weighting [30, 32]. If
generic IK priors and parameterizations fit users unevenly, an addi-
tional channel coupled to IK error may act as a confirmation signal
for some users, but may reinforce an incorrect mapping for others.
Another source of heterogeneity lies in the topology of the IK chain
itself. Topology refers to the functional role of each joint within
the reconstruction pipeline—whether it is directly constrained by
tracked sensors, inferred by the IK solver, or propagated through
the avatar hierarchy. Taken together, clarifying multimodal efficacy
requires moving beyond one-size-fits-all assumptions and asking
for whomandunder what conditionsspecific modality combina-
tions improve performance.
To address this question, we conducted a controlled experi-
ment (N=104) on bimanual upper-limb motor imitation in a
consumer VR setup. We compared four feedback conditions—
Visual-only (V), Visual+Audio (V A), Visual+Haptic (VH), and Vi-
sual+Audio+Haptic (V AH). Participants imitated pre-recorded ges-
tures under these conditions. We collected behavioral measures
(point-wise imitation accuracy) and offline recall performance to
evaluate learning outcomes, and subjective ratings (workload, mo-
tivation, presence, and embodiment) for exploratory analyses.
Our primary contributions are:
1.Empirical evidence for IK-chain topological limits, temporal
trade-offs, and user-moderated effects in IK-mediated mul-
timodal guidance:We find that multimodal efficacy is bounded

2 RELATEDWORK
Theories of motor skill learning increasingly emphasize embod-
ied cognition, where knowledge is grounded in sensorimotor in-
teraction and reenactment [23, 24, 4]. VR supports this learning-
by-doing paradigm through synthetic embodiment, aligning users
with avatar demonstrations to facilitate motor learning and trans-
fer [1, 21, 7]. However, this benefit is constrained by a fidelity
paradox. Adding sensory richness without sufficient physical accu-
racy can increase extraneous cognitive load [26, 15]. The issue is
particularly acute in sparse-tracking VR with IK, where reconstruc-
tion errors can introduce visuo-proprioceptive mismatches relative
to the user’s body schema [14].
2.1 Inverse Kinematics and Embodiment Constraints
Consumer VR systems typically track only a small number of 6-
DoF points (e.g. head and hands), making full-body reconstruction
mathematically underconstrained because of human kinematic re-
dundancy [22, 20]. In practice, heuristic IK solvers trade biome-
chanical fidelity for visual plausibility. However, priors used to
suppress implausible postures and reduce jitter can also introduce
metric distortions, decoupling avatar posture from the user’s pro-
prioceptive state [27].
During interaction, systems may further employ kinematic de-
coupling or pseudo-haptic techniques to evoke sensations such as
resistance [37]. Although effective for interaction design, such ma-
nipulations can induce a “follower effect,” in which users gradually
drift their physical limbs toward an erroneous avatar pose [17]. If
learners internalize motor patterns calibrated to a smoothed or spa-
tially biased IK representation, transfer to real-world execution may
degrade. To mitigate this risk, non-visual channels (e.g. audio and
haptics) have been explored as alternative carriers of error informa-
tion under imperfect visual embodiment [36, 10].
2.2 Multimodal Feedback and Sensory Integration
Multimodal augmentation in VR is commonly motivated by prob-
abilistic accounts of sensory integration, including maximum like-
lihood estimation (MLE) models [44]. When IK reduces visual fi-
delity, additional cues could in principle reduce perceptual uncer-
tainty by contributing independent, reliability-weighted estimates
of a shared latent state [5].
Different modalities offer distinct functional affordances. Hap-
tic feedback can stabilize body-centered perception; pseudo-haptic
cues, for example, can reduce overshoot by providing a reference
for mechanical impedance [16]. Movement sonification offers high
temporal resolution and can support rhythmic regulation while of-
floading part of the monitoring demand from vision [11], consistent
with resource-sharing accounts of attention [42].
However, multimodal gains are not guaranteed. Multiple Re-
source Theory predicts that cross-modal presentation can reduce
modality-specific bottlenecks, but coordination, integration, and re-
sponse selection still impose central processing costs [42]. More-
over, spatial misalignment or semantic inconsistency across cues
can introduce conflict-resolution costs that offset, or even outweigh,
the informational benefits of additional channels [41]. This tension
is especially relevant in IK-mediated VR, where multiple feedback
channels may be derived from the same solver-mediated error sig-
nal rather than from independent sensory estimates.
2.3 Gender Differences in Motor Control and Sensory
Processing
Prior work reports population-level variability in sensorimotor con-
trol, including group-level differences associated with sex/gender in
some task settings [30]. For example, the “crossed-hands deficit”
has been used to suggest that males may rely more on internal (ego-
centric) reference frames, whereas females may rely more on ex-
ternal (allocentric) visual frames in some spatial tasks [8]. Tactile
processing efficiency in males has also been linked to mental ro-
tation performance, suggesting a coupling between body-centered
cues and spatial processing [2]. In motor adaptation, some studies
further report that males may rely more on rapid, error-based strate-
gic calibration, whereas females may rely more on slower implicit
spatial alignment processes [25].
These differences may interact with VR hardware and embodi-
ment constraints. For instance, females have been reported to show
greater susceptibility to visually induced postural perturbations in
some settings [3]. Standard HMD configurations may also inad-
equately accommodate narrower interpupillary distances common
in female populations [35], and generic anthropometric priors in
kinematic models (e.g. shoulder-width assumptions) can introduce
systematic estimation error for some users [28, 40]. In addition,
modality-specific learning effects have been reported. In force-
control tasks, auditory feedback outperformed visual feedback for
females in some studies, whereas this advantage was not observed
for males [38]. Collectively, these findings motivate treating multi-
modal VR guidance not only as a signal-combination problem, but
also as a user-dependent adaptation problem.
3 METHODOLOGY
We conducted a between-subjects VR experiment to evaluate mul-
timodal guidance under IK-mediated embodiment. Specifically, we
tested the individual and combined effects of auditory and haptic
error cues added to a visual baseline [9], and examined moderation
by self-reported gender. The remainder of this section describes the
feedback design, tasks, experimental design, measurements, partic-
ipants, apparatus, and procedure. The Ethics Committee approved
this study with the protocol number (removed for review).

the learner’s and expert’s avatar joints to externalize moment-to-
moment spatial error. We augmented this baseline with auditory
and haptic channels that encode site-specific posture deviation with-
out increasing visual clutter.

3.1.1 Visual Feedback
We adopted the “puppet-based” visual linkage technique estab-
lished in prior work [9]. The visualization renders static, continu-

rent implementation, these visual linkages were applied to the same
14 upper-limb landmarks defined above (bilateral elbows, wrists,
and fingertips). For rendering details (e.g. fixed thickness and un-
modulated color), we refer readers to the original protocol [9].

3.2 Tasks
To elicit diverse upper-limb kinematics, we curated 12 station-
ary, gross-motor bimanual gestures from football referee signals 2.
These gestures span varying spatial extents, symmetry patterns, and
inter-limb coordination demands.
Crucially, during gesture selection we imposed a strict visibil-
ity constraint:both hands had to remain continuously within the
user’s field of view. This ensured uninterrupted access to the expert
demonstration and visual baseline, reducing uncontrolled variance
from occlusion and head turns.
3.3 Experiment
We examined how auxiliary auditory and haptic cues affect mo-
tor precision, short-term learning, and subjective experience during
IK-mediated embodiment. Specifically, we tested whether these
modalities act additively or interact with user factors across differ-
ent points in the kinematic chain.
The study used a 4 (Feedback)×2 (Gender) between-subjects
design (N=104; 26 per condition, gender-balanced via strati-
fied randomization). To capture adaptation dynamics across a di-
verse workspace, participants performed 12 actions×6 repetitions,
yielding kinematic deviation data across 14 upper-limb landmarks.
3.3.1 Conditions
Participants were assigned to one of four feedback configurations:
•Visual Only (V):Baseline condition with only the puppet-
string visual linkage (Section 3.1.1).
•Visual + Audio (V A):V plus stereo auditory sonification,
with pitch driven by the lateralized mean spatial error (Sec-
tion 3.1.2).
•Visual + Haptic (VH):V plus vibrotactile controller feed-
back, with vibration amplitude driven by the same lateralized
mean spatial error (Section 3.1.3).
•Visual + Audio + Haptic (V AH):Full multimodal combina-
tion (V + A + H), presented simultaneously to test modality-
stacking effects.

3.3.4 Participants
A total of 104 participants completed the experiment. Participants
were recruited from the university community (students and staff).
They were 18–32 years old (M=21.1,SD=2.5), including 52 self-
reported males and 52 self-reported females. Participants reported
occasional VR experience but no professional training.
Unlike prior work that required fixed participant anthropome-
try [9], we accommodated diverse body sizes via per-participant
avatar scaling, which was iteratively calibrated in VR to match each
participant’s perceived eye height and body proportions. All partic-
ipants reported normal or corrected-to-normal vision and no mus-
culoskeletal or cognitive impairments. All participants provided in-
formed consent prior to the experiment and received 20 CNY com-
pensation for their time.
3.3.5 Experiment Apparatus
The apparatus comprised a PC (Intel Core i7, RTX 4090, 128 GB
RAM) and an HTC Vive Pro HMD (2880×1600, 90 Hz) with stan-
dard controllers for tracking and vibrotactile output. The applica-
tion was developed in Unity 2021.3 using SteamVR and Final IK 3
to reconstruct upper-limb poses from sparse 3-point tracking (HMD
+ two controllers).
To reduce embodiment confounds, self-avatars and expert mod-
els (Customizable Survivors Pack 4) were matched to partici-
pants’ self-reported gender. Expert gestures were pre-recorded
by one male researcher and one female researcher. We inten-
tionally preserved naturalistic, demonstrator-specific kinematics
rather than forcing identical path geometries across demonstrators.
Within each participant gender group, the demonstrated motion set
was held constant across all feedback conditions. Accordingly,
between-gender differences should be interpreted as gender-group
effects under gender-matched demonstrations, rather than as purely
participant-intrinsic effects.
3.3.6 Procedure
Each session lasted approximately 60 minutes and consisted of
three phases:
A. Preparation:After consent and hardware fitting, participants
calibrated avatar scale to match their perceived body size and com-
pleted a three-gesture tutorial to familiarize themselves with the VR
environment and their assigned feedback cues.
B. Acquisition (Training):Participants practiced the 12 target
gestures under their assigned condition (6 randomized repetitions
per gesture; 72 trials total). Each trial began with a 5-second au-
ditory countdown and a 3-second on-screen text prompt indicating
the gesture name. Participants then continuously imitated the expert
avatar while kinematics were recorded.

4 RESULTS
We report objective kinematics, recall performance, and subjective
experience (FDR-corrected). Analyses of all subjective question-
naires (NASA-TLX, Motivation, IPQ, and Embodiment) revealed
no significant main effects or interactions (two-way ANOV As with
ConditionandGenderas factors; allp> .05). Thus, auxiliary
feedback did not produce statistically reliable differences in self-
reported workload, motivation, presence, or embodiment relative to
the visual baseline. The remainder of this section therefore focuses
on objective outcomes.Relative errors were log-transformed, and
model assumptions, including normality, were verified to be satis-
fied.
To improve generalizability across sparse-tracking embodi-
ment pipelines, we grouped the upper-limb chain into three IK-
topology categories rather than using a purely anatomical parti-
tion: (1)Sensor-Driven (Wrists), hardware-tracked 6-DoF con-
straints; (2)Solver-Driven (Elbows), IK-computed nodes that
satisfy chain constraints; and (3)Hierarchy-Driven (Fingers),
forward-kinematics–propagated nodes that amplify geometric de-
viations without participating in the IK solve. For analysis, we pro-
duced three group-level errors per participant×condition×action
×trial by aggregating joints within each category—per-joint rela-
tive errors were log-transformed and then averaged equally within
the group, yielding one scalar per group per trial.

5 DISCUSSION
This study examined whether augmenting visual guidance with au-
ditory sonification and vibrotactile feedback improves motor im-
itation under IK-mediated embodiment in consumer VR. The re-
sults do not support a simple modality-stacking account—adding
channels did not provide a uniform performance gain (H1 not sup-
ported), and haptic effects depended on both IK-topological joint
category and self-reported gender grouping (H2/H3 partially sup-
ported). In addition, visuo-haptic guidance (VH) accelerated on-
line adaptation (H5 partially supported), whereas the full stack
(V AH) did not preserve this advantage and was associated with
lower cued recall than the visual baseline (H7b not supported).

5.1 Kinematic Propagation and the “Amplifier Effect”
A central result is the joint-group dissociation in training
performance—feedback effects were absent at elbows, weak at
wrists, and strongest at fingers. This pattern is consistent with the
algorithmic structure of the IK chain and the physical locus of the
haptic stimulus.
In our setup, the wrist is asensor-drivenjoint directly con-
strained by the controller’s tracked 6-DoF pose. The elbow is a
solver-drivenjoint whose configuration is inferred by the IK solver
(e.g. geometric constraints and bend heuristics) rather than directly
measured. Because vibrotactile feedback was delivered through the
controllers, it provided localized information at the hand-held end
effector but no direct information about the elbow’s latent IK so-
lution. Under this configuration, elbow behavior is dominated by
solver priors and chain constraints, which plausibly explains why
elbow-level DID contrasts remained non-significant.
By contrast, the fingers arehierarchy-drivennodes whose fin-
gertip positions are ultimately determined by upstream hand/wrist
pose through the avatar’s forward-kinematic chain. As a result, dis-
tal endpoints can exhibitamplifiedpositional changes. That means,
relatively small wrist/hand adjustments may result in larger dis-
placements at the fingertips due to lever-arm effects along the distal
chain. Importantly, the pronounced gender divergence we observed
at the fingers under haptic-involving contrasts does not by itself
establish digit-specific control strategies. Instead, it iscompatible
withan account in which wrist-localized corrections (or hand-level
pose changes) propagate downstream and become most visible at
the distal end effector.
5.2 Gender-Specific Processing of Localized Haptics
The distal DID results further suggest that the same controller-
localized haptic signal was not functionally equivalent across users.
At the finger level, contrasts involving haptics (VH–V A; V AH–V A)
were significant, indicating that haptic inclusion benefited the male
group more than the female group (H2/H3 partially supported).
At the wrist level, the same pattern appeared only as a numerical
trend. In contrast, adding audio on top of haptics (V AH–VH) did
not produce a reliable DID effect in any joint group (H4 not sup-
ported).
A possible interpretation is that controller vibration acted as a
state-confirmation cuefor some users but asinterferencefor others.
Because vibration is co-located with the tracked end effector, it may
help users verify wrist state (position/timing) when the cue aligns
with their preferred integration strategy. Conversely, if a user re-
lies more strongly on visual flow or auditory rhythm, an additional
hand-localized tactile cue may compete for attention or be treated
as noise, especially when the cue is abstract (error-coded) rather

5.3 Visuo-Haptic Feedback as a Learning Catalyst
The learning-rate analysis clarifies the functional role of haptics.
VH did not simply lower error uniformly. Instead, it increased
the rate of error reduction across trials relative to the visual base-
line (H5 partially supported). This pattern supports interpreting
visuo-haptic guidance as alearning catalyst. To explain, VH can
accelerate short-term calibration even when no global main effect
of condition is present.
One interpretation is that low-latency coupling between vi-
sual discrepancy and wrist-localized tactile feedback increases the
salience of actionable error during practice, enabling faster trial-
to-trial updating. However, this catalytic effect did not extend to
the full multimodal stack. V AH showed a shallower learning slope
than VH and no reliable advantage over V , indicating that a third
channel did not produce additive gains. This is compatible with
split-attention or coordination costs in multimodal monitoring [42]:
even when signals are distributed across modalities, users must still
allocate attention, reconcile cue meaning, and select corrective ac-
tions in real time.
We also did not observe a significantCondition×Gender×Trial
interaction (H6 not supported). Thus, although gender-group dif-

5.4 Retention Trade-offs under Stacked Multimodal
Guidance
The recall resultssuggestan execution–retention trade-off under
stacked guidance. Feedback condition did not affect free recall
(H7a not supported), but cued recall showed a significant main
effect of condition, with the visual baseline outperforming V AH
(contrary to H7b). In other words, the condition with the rich-
est concurrent guidance did not yield the strongest semantic–motor
retrieval.
This pattern is consistent with theGuidance Hypothesis[33]:
dense augmented feedback can support practice performance while
weakening performance once those cues are removed. In the
present task, V AH may have encouraged reliance on external cor-
rection signals during execution without equally strengthening the
internal semantic–motor mapping required for cued retrieval. No-
tably, subjective questionnaires did not reveal reliable differences
(e.g. workload or embodiment). Two possible explanations are: (1)
the retention cost may not directly translate into subjective experi-
ence during practice; and (2) the retention cost may not be readily
captured by self-report, instead requiring behavioral memory mea-
sures to detect. Future work could probe this dissociation with de-
layed recall/transfer tests or more sensitive metacognitive measures.
5.5 Design Guidelines for Multimodal IK Training
For consumer VR training systems that rely on IK to reconstruct
upper-limb motion, our results indicate that adding more feedback
channels does not necessarily improve learning. Instead, multi-
modal effectiveness is bounded by the avatar’s algorithmic topol-
ogy, the temporal phase of skill acquisition, and user-specific sen-
sorimotor response profiles. Based on these findings, we propose
three engineering-oriented guidelines for balancing online kine-
matic support and short-term retention.
Guideline 1: Respect Solver Topology (Masked Haptics)—
motivated by Section 5.1.Map feedback only to joints for which
users have direct or behaviorally meaningful control authority. In
our data, controller-coupled haptics did not reliably improve elbow-
level performance, whereas effects emerged at the wrist/finger end
of the chain.
•Topology mismatch.In common VR IK pipelines, elbow pose
is a solver-determined latent variable inferred from distal con-
straints (e.g. tracked hand pose) and bend heuristics. Trigger-
ing controller vibration for elbow-related deviation creates a
control mismatch: the signal is delivered at the hand, but the
user cannot directly manipulate the solver’s proximal latent
variables to resolve elbow error.
•Implementation rule.Apply a topology-aware error mask be-
fore issuing haptic feedback. If the deviation is primarily at-
tributable to solver-dominated proximal variables (e.g. elbow
bend/swivel ambiguity), suppress controller vibration and rely
on visual disambiguation (e.g. linkages or ghost overlays).
Restrict haptic signaling to sensor-driven joints (wrists in this
study) and hierarchy-driven distal segments (fingers), where
the cue remains actionable and behaviorally observable.
Guideline 2: Detect Sensory Interference Early (Adap-
tive Profiling)—motivated by Section 5.2.The distalCondi-
tion×Genderinteraction indicates that the same haptic mapping
can help some users and hinder others. Accordingly, modality as-
signment should be behavior-driven rather than fixed.
•Rationale.Do not use demographic labels as proxies for sen-
sory preference. Instead, estimate each user’s response profile
directly from early behavioral performance.
•Calibration protocol.Add a brief onboarding probe (e.g. 20–
30 s per block) comparing V A and VH under matched task
difficulty.
•Decision logic.Monitor distal endpoint error (e.g.
wrist/finger relative error). If adding haptics reduces error rel-
ative to V A, retain VH. If haptics increase error, classify the
response as likely sensory interference and switch to V A or V .
Re-evaluate periodically, as the optimal profile may change
over learning.
Guideline 3: Use Scaffold-and-Wean Scheduling—motivated
by Section 5.3.The combination of faster online adaptation in VH
and poorer cued recall in V AH suggests a guidance trade-off: dense
feedback can accelerate early calibration, but sustained stacking
may increase dependence and weaken retention.
•Phase 1: Acquisition (scaffold).During early practice, prior-
itize visuo-haptic (VH) feedback. In our study, VH produced
the steepest learning slope, indicating faster trial-to-trial cali-
bration.
•Phase 2: Consolidation (wean).Avoid persistent V AH as the
default. Once performance begins to plateau, progressively
fade haptic and/or auditory channels. This shifts users from
externally guided correction toward internally generated exe-
cution, which is more compatible with semantic–motor reten-
tion.
6 LIMITATIONS
This study has several limitations. First, demonstrator identity was
nested within gender to preserve naturalistic motion, which im-
proves ecological validity but means absolute gender main effects
may partly reflect demonstrator-specific motion statistics; there-
fore, within-gender condition contrasts provide the strongest evi-
dence for modality effects. Second, the findings are specific to IK-
mediated embodiment under sparse 6-DoF tracking and may not
generalize to other embodiment pipelines (e.g. full-body motion
capture or alternative IK solvers). Third, the study used standing,
stationary, bimanual gross-motor gestures in a single session with
immediate recall, so the results do not establish longer-term reten-
tion or transfer to other task classes (e.g. fine manipulation or loco-
motion). Fourth, recall scoring was performed by a single blinded
rater based on gesture identifiability, and inter-rater reliability was
not assessed. Finally, the auditory and haptic channels used a sin-
gle heuristic cue parameterization, and different mappings or gain
schedules may alter the observed balance between facilitation and
interference.
7 CONCLUSION
We investigated multimodal guidance in IK-mediated VR motor
imitation and found that its effectiveness is bounded by both IK
topology and a trade-off between online execution and short-term
retention. Multimodal effects were localized to sensor-driven and
hierarchy-driven segments (wrists/fingers), while solver-driven el-
bows showed little sensitivity to auxiliary cues. Visuo-Haptic (VH)
guidance accelerated early adaptation, but this benefit did not per-
sist under the full Visuo-Audio-Haptic (V AH) stack, which was also
associated with poorer cued recall than the visual baseline.
We also observed user-group moderation in distal performance:
the same haptic mapping improved accuracy for one group but de-
graded it for another, indicating that modality utility is not uniform
across users. Together, these results challenge a “more feedback is
better” assumption for consumer VR training under sparse-tracking
embodiment. Instead, our findings support topology-aware, adap-
tive multimodal design. We distill this into three practical principles
for IK-mediated training:Topology-Aware Masking,Interference
Detection, andScaffold-and-Weanscheduling.
