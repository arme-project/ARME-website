Error-Related Eye Response: Error Detection and Confidence Support in
Upper Limb Virtual Reality Rehabilitation
I.OBJECTIVE MEASURES
A.Eye response feature statistics
Eye response feature statistics quantify objective, window-level changes in eye behaviour that
characterise Error-Related Eye Responses (ErrE) during the exergame/therapy task.Eye tracking signals
were analysed continuously using a sliding window of lengthTms updated at 10 Hz. For analysis, windows
were grouped intoNormalandErrorclasses according to the study protocol (i.e.,Errorwindows correspond
to perceived error instances, whereasNormalwindows correspond to error-free interaction windows). For
each class and window length, descriptive statistics were computed for each feature to provide an objective
description of ErrE patterns and to support interpretation of the detection results.
The following eye-derived features were extracted from each window:
•Eye Blinks.The number of blinks detected within the window. A blink was defined as a complete
closure–reopening cycle in the eye-openness signal: the eye openness value drops to0%(closure) and
subsequently returns above0%(reopening). Each such closure–reopening event was counted as one
blink. This feature captures transient visual interruption that may occur when a perceived error elicits a
reflexive blink response.
•Gaze Movement.The number of rapid gaze shifts within the window. A saccade was defined as a discrete
gaze movement whose angular displacement exceeded a predefined threshold (e.g.,≥7 ◦) within a short
time interval, indicating a rapid redirection of gaze rather than slow drift. This feature captures abrupt
gaze reorientation that may occur when a user notices an unexpected outcome and visually checks the
scene or the manipulated object.
•Pupil size change (%).The percentage change in pupil size from the start to the end of the window,
computed as
∆P(%) = Pend −P start
Pstart +ε ×100,
whereP start andP end denote pupil diameter at the beginning and end of the window. This feature captures
rapid pupillary dynamics that may reflect an arousal-like response to unexpected events.
•Eye openness change (%).The percentage change in eye openness from the start to the end of the
window, computed as
∆O(%) = Oend −O start
Ostart +ε ×100,
whereO start andO end denote the eye openness value at the beginning and end of the window. This
feature captures brief eyelid aperture changes that may accompany perceived error responses.
A separate explanation of the event-aligned schematic eye-response visualisation, including the signed
normalised gaze-shift trace, is provided in Appendix I-C.
B.Task performance
Task performance summarises how efficiently and reliably participants completed the reach–grasp–place
task using two simple behavioural indices: mean completion time per trial and error frequency per
trial.Each participant performed the task continuously until the session ended once sufficient error instances
had been recorded. Because the number of completed trials therefore varied across participants and conditions,
performance was expressed using per-trial averages that remain comparable despite different session lengths.
The following measures were computed for each participant and each condition:

C.Construction and interpretation of schematic eye-response traces
This subsection clarifies the event-aligned eye-response traces used for visual interpretation of Error-Related
Eye Responses (ErrE). These traces were introduced to provide an intuitive temporal summary of how eye
behaviour changes before and after a perceived error event. They should be interpreted separately from the
window-level statistical features described in Appendix I-A, which were the actual features used for analysis
and detection.
Relation to the main ErrE features.The features used by ErrE for statistical analysis were computed
on sliding windows and included blink count, gaze-movement count, pupil-size change, and eye-openness
change. By contrast, the event-aligned traces presented in the schematic visualisation were constructed as
time-resolved descriptive signals to illustrate the typical temporal evolution of eye behaviour around an error
event. Accordingly, these traces were intended for interpretation and visual explanation rather than for direct
model fitting.
Event-centred time axis.All schematic traces were aligned to the perceived error onset att= 0ms. Negative
time values denote behaviour observed before the error became apparent, whereas positive time values denote
behaviour observed after the error. The full illustrative window spanned from−500ms to1000ms, with
values sampled at 10 ms intervals.
Eye openness and pupil size.For visual consistency, eye openness and pupil diameter were expressed as
percentage-change signals across the event-centred window. Positive values indicate an increase relative to the
local reference state, whereas negative values indicate a decrease. In the schematic trace, these signals were
used to visualise the qualitative pattern observed during ErrE, including pre-error blink-related eye closure,
post-error widening of eye openness, and delayed pupil dilation.
Blink event.Blink was represented as a binary temporal state rather than as a continuous percentage-valued
signal. A time segment was labelled asBlinkwhen the eyelid was considered to be within a blink interval, and
asNootherwise. In the visual plot, blink was represented as a shaded temporal window in the background,
so that the continuous percentage-valued signals could remain clearly visible.
Normalised gaze shift (%).To visualise the direction and strength of gaze reorientation around the error
event, a signed gaze-shift quantity was defined relative to two task-relevant spatial regions: the hand side and
the object/error side. Letx(t)denote the gaze location at timet, and letc hand andc obj denote the centres of
the hand region and the object/error region, respectively. The corresponding distances are
dhand(t) =∥x(t)−c hand∥, d obj(t) =∥x(t)−c obj∥.
A signed gaze-shift score was then defined as
g(t) =d hand(t)−d obj(t).
Under this definition, a positive value ofg(t)indicates that gaze is closer to the object or error location
than to the hand, whereas a negative value indicates that gaze is closer to the hand side than to the object
side. A value near zero indicates relatively stable fixation or an approximately balanced position between the
two regions.

Interpretation of the sign.The sign convention was defined explicitly for interpretability:
•G(t)>0: gaze shifts towards the object or error location;
•G(t)<0: gaze shifts towards the hand or limb side;
•G(t)≈0: gaze remains relatively stable or lies between the two task-relevant regions.
The absolute magnitude|G(t)|reflects the strength of the directional shift. Larger absolute values correspond
to stronger gaze reorientation, whereas smaller absolute values correspond to weaker directional bias.
Interpretive purpose.This signed, normalised gaze-shift signal was used to make the temporal structure
of ErrE easier to interpret visually. In particular, it helps illustrate the rapid hand-object checking behaviour
before an error, the immediate post-error refocusing towards the dropped object or error location, and the
later scanning behaviour that follows the error.

II.SUBJECTIVEMEASURES
A.Confidence
Confidence was assessed using a task-adapted Perceived Competence Scale (PCS), a brief self-report
measure grounded in Self-Determination Theory and commonly used to capture perceived competence
for a specific activity [1].The PCS was adapted to the present exergame/therapy task by phrasing each
item with explicit reference to the immediately preceding gameplay block (with ErrE or without ErrE). Each
item was rated on a 7-point Likert scale (1 = Strongly Disagree, 7 = Strongly Agree). Higher scores indicate
greater perceived competence (task confidence) while performing the exergame/therapy task.
Participants responded to the following items after each condition:
1) I felt confident that I could perform the tasks in this exergame/therapy successfully.
2) I felt capable of meeting the requirements of this exergame/therapy task.
3) I felt able to achieve the goals of this exergame/therapy task.
4) I felt able to cope with the difficulty level of this exergame/therapy task.
A composite confidence score was computed by averaging the four item responses and converting the result
to a percentage, yielding a score between 0% and 100%, where higher values indicate higher confidence:
Confidence Score (%)=

B.Usability
The System Usability Scale (SUS) is a widely used instrument for evaluating perceived usability [2].
It consists of 10 items alternating between positive and negative phrasing, each rated on a 5-point Likert scale
(1 = Strongly Disagree, 5 = Strongly Agree). The SUS is designed to provide a quick yet reliable measure
of how easy and pleasant a system is to use, covering aspects such as complexity, consistency, confidence,
and learnability.
Participants responded to the following items:
1) I think that I would like to use this system frequently.
2) I found the system unnecessarily complex.
3) I thought the system was easy to use.
4) I think that I would need the support of a technical person to be able to use this system.
5) I found the various functions in this system were well integrated.
6) I thought there was too much inconsistency in this system.
7) I would imagine that most people would learn to use this system very quickly.
8) I found the system very cumbersome to use.
9) I felt very confident using the system.
10) I needed to learn a lot of things before I could get going with this system.
The SUS score is calculated using the standard formula. A higher SUS score indicates better perceived
usability, with scores above 68 generally considered above average.

C.Engagement
The Hopkins Rehabilitation Engagement Rating Scale (HRERS) is a validated clinical tool used
to assess patient engagement in rehabilitation settings [3].It evaluates key behaviours such as attitude,
participation, and perceived value of therapy.
This research adapted the original clinician-rated version into a participant-facing, self-report format suitable
for therapy/exergame contexts. The item on attendance was replaced with a measure of willingness, as
participants attended only one session. Each item was rated on a 6-point Likert scale (1 = Strongly Disagree,
6 = Strongly Agree), with one item (Q2) reverse-scored to account for prompt dependency. The modified
questions are:
1) I would be willing to participate in this exergame/therapy again.
2) I required verbal or physical prompts to actively participate in my exergame/therapy activity.
3) I feel positive about my exergame/therapy activities.
4) I recognise the need for and benefits of engaging in exergame/therapy activities.
5) I actively engage and put effort into my exergame/therapy activities.
The final engagement score is calculated using the following formula. This yields a percentage between
0% and 100%, with higher values indicating stronger engagement in the rehabilitation task.
Engagement Score (%)=
(Q1 + (7−Q 2) +Q 3 +Q 4 +Q 5)−5

D.User Experience
User Experience was assessed using a brief 3-item questionnaire targeting three core dimensions
relevant to exergame and therapeutic engagement: motivation, confidence, and satisfaction. Each question
was adapted from a validated instrument and reworded for use in the VR rehabilitation context. The questionnaire’s
question picks the core idea question from each classic questionnaire.
Participants rated their agreement with each item on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = Strongly Disagree, 5 =
Strongly Agree):
1)Motivation[4]: I feel that doing the tasks in this exergame/therapy is fun.
2)Confidence[5]: I feel confident to do the tasks well in this exercise/therapy.
3)Satisfaction[2]: I am satisfied with this exergame/therapy.
The final user experience score is calculated by averaging the responses and converting it into a percentage.

E.Clinician Observation
Clinician Observation is a clinician-completed questionnaire designed to assess a patient’s engagement
and progress during game-based rehabilitation compared to traditional therapy.It captures expert
impressions across five key domains:
1)Movement Strength:I feel that the patient exhibits stronger movements when engaging with the game
compared to traditional rehabilitation exercises.
2)Flexibility:I have noticed an improvement in the patient’s flexibility or range of motion with game-based
rehabilitation.
3)Joyfulness:The patient appears more joyful and shows a more positive attitude during the game-based
sessions.
4)Engagement:The game has increased the patient’s interest and active involvement in the rehabilitation
process.
