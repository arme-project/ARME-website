When Registration Error Becomes Ambiguous: Behavioural Effects of
Static Misalignment in Augmented Reality
Category: Research
ABSTRACT
Static registration error can impair user performance in augmented
reality, but its behavioural and subjective effects remain insuffi-
ciently characterised. While existing works often evaluate accept-
able registration quality as a purely quantitative geometric prob-
lem, this perspective overlooks how spatial misalignment creates
perceptual ambiguity for the user. In this paper, we demonstrate
that AR registration requirements might not be defined by abso-
lute offset magnitude alone, but also by the physical layout and the
boundaries of cue-target ambiguity. We report a controlled video
see-through augmented reality study with 24 participants using a re-
producible 3 × 3 button-acquisition task, in which a visual cue was
displaced relative to physical targets across six offset magnitudes.
User performance remained close to the zero-offset reference at the
smallest offsets, but degraded significantly under larger misalign-
ments. Participants also showed short-term adaptation after the
switch to misalignment, although larger offsets retained substan-
tial residual costs. Subjective ratings showed parallel increases in
rating with performance. These findings provide a basis for setting
task-specific registration requirements by identifying offset ranges
that remain behaviourally tolerable, rather than those that produce
ambiguity-driven breakdown under explicit performance criteria.
Index Terms:AR, Registration Error, Reaction Time.
1 INTRODUCTION
Augmented Reality (AR) overlays virtual content onto the real
world in order to support perception and action. A core require-
ment for such overlays is spatial registration, that is, the alignment
and synchronisation of virtual cues with their corresponding physi-
cal referents or real-world events [2, 22, 26]. Its usefulness depends
not only on the presence of an augmentation, but also on whether
the displayed cue preserves a meaningful spatial relationship to the
physical referent [22, 26]. When registration error is low relative to
the spatial tolerance of the task, virtual cues can be interpreted di-
rectly in relation to the workspace. As registration error increases,
the cue may remain visible but become a less reliable actionable
indicator of the intended physical referent.
In practice, perfect alignment is difficult to obtain. Tracking
limitations, such as calibration error, modelling inaccuracies, and
system latency, can all introduce spatial deviation between virtual
content and the physical scene. This deviation is commonly de-
scribed as registration error and remains a central challenge in AR
systems [20]. Although registration error is often discussed as a
system-accuracy problem, its consequences for interaction depend
on how misalignment affects the user’s interpretation of the cue dur-
ing task performance [3, 26].
In discrete spatial tasks, increasing static registration error need
not act only as a monotonic increase in difficulty. Instead, it may
alter the functional relationship between the virtual cue and the in-
tended physical target. At small offsets, the cue may still preserve a
clear association with a single target. At intermediate offsets, espe-
cially near the midpoint between neighbouring targets, the cue may
become ambiguous[10, 27]. At larger offsets, it may no longer ap-
pear locally attached to the target and may instead be interpreted as
an external spatial reference that requires deliberate remapping[34].
Under this view, static misalignment is not only a matter of reduced
spatial accuracy, but also a matter of weakened cue–target associa-
tion and changing perceptual interpretation [15, 23, 34].
This distinction is likely to matter for behaviour. If the displaced
cue still specifies one target clearly, users may remain relatively
efficient despite some error. Once ambiguity emerges, however,
users may require additional time to interpret the cue before initi-
ating movement, and may also show more incorrect selections or
prolonged movement execution. Separating these effects is im-
portant because delays before movement onset and delays during
movement execution may reflect different underlying processes, in-
cluding cue interpretation, response selection, and online correc-
tion [19, 36, 44, 46]. For this reason, acceptable registration quality
for a given task should not be defined by absolute offset alone, but
by how offset interacts with target spacing, action demands, and
explicit behavioural criteria such as time cost and error rate.
Motivated by this issue, we investigate how increasing static reg-
istration error affects performance and subjective experience in a
rapid AR target-acquisition task. We use a controlled video see-
through setup and a reproducible 3×3 button-acquisition task in
which a visual cue is horizontally displaced relative to physical tar-
gets across multiple offset magnitudes chosen with respect to target
spacing. This enables millisecond-level measurement of reaction
time, movement time, and correctness, together with post-condition
ratings of workload, perceived difficulty, and interaction quality.
The paper addresses the following question: how does increasing
static registration error affect objective performance and subjective
response in a discrete AR target-acquisition task, and what does this
imply for task-specific registration requirements? We make three
contributions. First, we introduce a controlled and reproducible
task paradigm for studying static registration error in AR. Sec-
ond, we characterise how increasing offset affects response initi-
ation, movement execution, correctness, and short-term adaptation
across offsets spanning clear-association, ambiguity, and detached-
cue regimes. Third, we complement these behavioural measures
with questionnaire-based ratings of workload, perceived difficulty,
and interaction quality, providing an integrated evaluation frame-
work for studying registration error under this class of task.
2 BACKGROUND
2.1 Target Acquisition and Task Design
Target acquisition tasks provide a controlled way to study how
spatial properties of an interface affect action time and accuracy.
Fitts’ law remains the classic framework for relating performance
to movement amplitude and target width in aimed movement [12].
In the present work, we draw on this tradition not to claim a canon-
ical ISO Fitts task, but to motivate a controlled target-acquisition
paradigm for a discrete physical layout.
In button-based interfaces, increasing target size generally re-
duces misses, errors, and completion time, whereas the effects of
inter-target spacing are less consistent and appear to depend on the
overall layout [8, 17]. These considerations are especially impor-
tant in Extended Reality (XR), where Fitts-style studies vary sub-
stantially in task design and reporting [1]. Roig-Maim ´o et al. fur-
ther showed that non-ISO target-selection tasks can produce less
interpretable throughput estimates when target arrangement and
movement amplitude vary excessively within a sequence [35]. Ac-
cordingly, we use large buttons and generous spacing to minimise

2.2 Static Misalignment as a Perceptual and Be-
havioural Problem
Registration error in AR is often introduced as a problem of track-
ing, calibration, and spatial alignment. However, earlier discussions
of perceptual issues in AR make clear that the effectiveness of an
augmentation depends not only on nominal geometric placement,
but also on how virtual content is perceived and interpreted relative
to the physical scene [26]. In goal-directed action, visual, propri-
oceptive, and haptic information are combined according to their
relative reliability [11, 48, 43]. Static registration error therefore
does not simply reduce overlay accuracy. It can also weaken the
cue–target relationship by making the displaced visual cue a less
reliable indicator of the intended physical referent.
In a discrete control layout, increasing offset may alter not only
spatial accuracy but also how the cue is interpreted. We use three
perceptual regimes as a conceptual framework for this progres-
sion: aclear-associationregime, in which the cue still unambigu-
ously indicates the intended target; anambiguityregime, in which it
could plausibly refer to multiple nearby targets; and adetached-cue
regime, in which it no longer appears locally attached to the lay-
out and must instead be interpreted as an external spatial reference.
This framing suggests that the effect of misalignment may become
non-linear once the displaced cue no longer specifies a unique tar-
get reliably, even though the formal hypotheses concern monotonic
degradation with increasing offset magnitude.
2.3 Response Timing and Adaptation
Performance in aimed action is commonly understood as the out-
come of multiple processing stages rather than a single undiffer-
entiated response latency. Classic reaction-time accounts treat the
interval between stimulus onset and overt action as reflecting pre-
movement processes such as cue encoding, response selection, and
movement preparation [44, 19]. Related work further suggests that
movement control can continue during execution through online ad-
justment [36, 46]. In visually guided reaching, target information
can be used online during movement, and reach trajectories can be
corrected rapidly in response to target displacement [31, 30, 14].
Static registration error may therefore impair performance in at
least two ways. It may delay interpretation of the displaced cue
and preparation of the intended response before movement begins,
and it may increase the need for corrective control once the reach
has been initiated. Analysing reaction time and movement time
separately is therefore more informative than relying on a single
aggregate completion-time measure. Because the offset within a
condition is stable rather than random, participants may also par-
tially adapt over repeated trials by recalibrating their mapping from
cue to target or by adopting compensatory strategies [25, 32, 24].
2.4 Prior Studies of Registration Error
Prior AR and XR studies suggest that spatial distortion affects not
only task performance, but also how users interpret and rely on aug-
mented cues during action. Robertson et al. examined static regis-
tration error in a tabletop AR Lego block-placement task by com-
paring no error, fixed translation error, and random translation error,
both with and without added graphical context. Using time, place-
ment errors, and questionnaire responses, they showed that partici-
pants gradually adapted to fixed error over repeated trials, whereas
random error without context reduced the task to near-guessing.
Added context reduced placement errors under misalignment, al-
though it could also increase completion time because participants
had to interpret additional spatial information [34]. Gottsacker et
al. examined simulated tracking errors in an optical see-through AR
search-and-selection task. Their study separated fixed accuracy off-
sets from dynamic precision jitter, showing that even small angular
errors affected both task performance and subjective trust, particu-
larly when participants relied primarily on AR cues [15].
A related perspective is provided by Killough et al., who stud-
ied imposed spatial offset in a HoloLens 2 task involving writing
and walking. Although framed as drift tolerance, their conditions
were implemented as fixed offsets and evaluated using behavioural
performance, Likert ratings, and qualitative reports. In the static
writing task, performance and perceived difficulty worsened as off-
set increased, with clearer degradation at larger offsets, and partic-
ipants differed in whether they followed the displayed drifted loca-
tion directly or attempted to compensate for it [23].
Taken together, these studies show that spatial distortion in AR
and XR affects performance, trust, and user strategy, but they do
not yet characterise how increasing fixed translational offset affects
rapid discrete acquisition of real controls when the cost of target in-
terpretation can be separated from the cost of movement execution.
2.5 Reaction Time and Measures in MR
There has been a noted relationship between presence and reaction
time in MR where reaction time could predict presence [6, 7, 5]. In
virtual reality (VR), presence is often defined as the “sense of being
there” [39]. In MR, however, there is some debate as to whether
presence exists in the same manner as it does in VR, with competing
definitions and metrics. This is because presence in VR requires a
virtual environment (VE) [42] and, as MR takes place in the real
world, MR does not have a VE. This raises a problem when trying
to measure presence in MR.
When using reaction time as a proxy for presence, Chandio et
al. used unaltered VR presence questionnaires to try and measure
the experience of presence in MR [6]. This creates a problem as
unaltered VR presence questionnaires have not been validated for
MR [47]. There is concern that, because presence lacks a VE, these
questionnaires are not applicable as many of them ask users to com-
pare their experience of the virtual world to their experience of the
physical world. As such, the authors cannot definitively say that
they used reaction time as a proxy for presence. Further complicat-
ing matters, there is a lack of proof suggesting objective measures,
such as reaction time, are adequate or reliable measures of presence,
especially as presence is considered to be personal that depends on
the user and their interpretation of the experience [47, 16].
Despite the doubts surrounding presence, this study and its find-
ings regarding reaction time should not be ignored. Clearly, re-
action time was measurable and correlated to something, even if
that was not specifically presence. Perhaps what Chandio et al.
were measuring was not presence, but engagement or a similar phe-
nomenon. While not yet studied in MR, a relationship between en-
gagement and reaction time has been noted in the field of game and
education [21, 28]. Reaction time as an objective measure for user
experience should be more deeply considered.
3 RESEARCHAIM ANDHYPOTHESES
We investigate how increasing static registration error affects objec-
tive performance and subjective response in a discrete AR target-
acquisition task, and what this implies for task-specific registration
requirements. Rather than treating acceptable error as a purely ge-
ometric quantity, we examine it with respect to explicit behavioural
criteria. We also examine whether increasing registration error is
reflected in subjective ratings collected after each condition.

4 METHOD
4.1 Participants
Twenty-four volunteers (10 female, 14 male; estimatedM age =
28.8,SD=6.3) were recruited from the university community.
Age was collected in 10-year intervals due to ethics requirements,
and the reported mean and standard deviation were estimated using
the midpoint of each age range. All participants reported normal
or corrected-to-normal vision and no history of epileptic episodes.
The study was approved by the institutional ethics committee, and
written informed consent was obtained from every participant.
4.2 Apparatus
The experimental system comprised a Meta Quest 3 video see-
through mixed-reality headset and a custom control panel mounted
on a height-adjustable table (Figure 1 (b)) with a USB-serial inter-
face, and a host PC. The wooden control panel is 60×60 cm with
nine circular tactile buttons (diameter 100 mm) arranged in a 3×3
grid with 5 cm edge-to-edge spacing (15 cm centre-to-centre spac-
ing). A Unity 2022.3 application using Meta SDK v85 rendered
the virtual overlays in the headset, while the host PC managed the
experiment logic and controlled the virtual LED cues. Communica-
tion between the PC and headset was implemented over Wi-Fi using
a TCP-based messaging framework. Details on parts and construc-
tion can be provided upon request.
Participants interacted with a wooden control panel. The dome-
shaped button top provides a clear tactile cue about the centre of the
button. Each button contained an individually addressable white
LED beneath a flat diffuser as a real light stimulus, allowing the
same physical layout to be used for both real and virtual cue pre-
sentation. The virtual LED texture was derived from a photograph
of an illuminated physical button in order to approximate the halo
produced by the diffuser. Brightness was adjusted to reduce visual
mismatch, although the rendered cue remained visually distinguish-
able from the physical LED from differences in display contrast and
pass-through capture.
We used a video see-through (VST) headset rather than an opti-
cal see-through display in order to reduce accommodation-related
confounds when switching attention between virtual cues and the
physical workspace [13]. For VST headsets, the pass-through im-
age and virtual augmentation are rendered on the same display
plane, which makes the attentional and perceptual effects of spa-
tial misalignment easier to interpret. Their wider field of view also
ensured that the largest imposed offsets remained visible rather than
being clipped by the display boundary.
For spatial registration, the virtual LED panel was aligned with
the physical control panel using four QR codes, one at each corner
of the board. The Meta MRUK QR Code Trackable is spawned for
each QR code (example scene in Figure 1 (b)), and a Spatial An-
chor is spawned from each trackable to stabilise the virtual panel
over the session. Alignment was visually checked (under around 1
cm) before each session and recalibrated when necessary. This ap-
proach provided more consistent placement than manual alignment,
particularly after table-height adjustment.
4.3 Task
We employed a discrete target-acquisition task with controllable
static registration error to test how misalignment magnitude af-
fected rapid responding to physical controls.
On each trial, participants responded to a single illuminated tar-
get on a 3×3 button board (control panel). Depending on the
condition, the cue was presented either as a physical LED on the
corresponding button (Baseline) or as a virtual LED rendered in the
headset (Control and Offset). Participants were instructed to tap the
corresponding physical button as accurately and as quickly as possi-
ble. Each trial began when the participant placed both hands on the
A and B buttons of a Microsoft Adaptive Controller, which served
as the home position and triggered trial onset. After a randomised
holding interval, one target cue was presented. Participants con-
tinued holding the home-position buttons until they initiated their
response by moving one hand to tap the cued button. This variable
foreperiod reduced anticipatory responses to predictable cue onset,
as illustrated in Figure 1 (c).
In the Offset conditions, the visual cue was laterally displaced
relative to the physical button layout. Participants therefore had to
interpret the misaligned cue and reach to the intended physical tar-
get despite the altered cue-target relationship. After each tap, the
LED corresponding to the pressed button was illuminated as re-
sponse feedback. If the participant pressed an incorrect button, the
original cue was turned off immediately and the pressed button was
illuminated instead, which could serve as an immediate correction
cue. A rising sound was played for a correct response and a falling
sound was played for an incorrect response.
4.4 Experimental Design
Conditions and Trial StructureThe study used a within-
subjects design with the static Registration Error (RE) magnitude
as the independent variable. As shown in Fig. 1, the study com-
prised eight conditions: Baseline (physical LEDs only), Control
(AR overlay with 0cm offset), and six Offset conditions with lat-
eral misalignment magnitudes of 5, 7.5, 11.25, 22.5, 35, and 40cm.
The button layout had a centre-to-centre spacing of 15cm. The
error magnitudes were chosen to probe theoretically meaningful po-
sitions relative to this spacing, from small displacements that pre-
serve a clear cue-target association, through ambiguous intermedi-
ate offsets, to a large displacement that places the cue outside the
board while remaining visible in the headset field of view. The
5 cm condition represents a small displacement that remains be-
low the midpoint between adjacent buttons. The 7.5 cm condition
places the virtual LED midway between two adjacent buttons, cor-
responding to 0.5×the spacing and marking the onset of percep-
tual ambiguity. The 11.25 cm condition corresponds to 0.75×the
spacing and was included to further probe this ambiguity before a
full one-column shift. The 22.5 cm condition corresponds to 1.5×
the spacing, placing the virtual LED at an equal-distance confusion
point after a one-column shift. The 35 cm condition applies a two-
column shift plus less than 0.5×the spacing, keeping the cue within
the board extent while preserving a similar ambiguity pattern. Fi-
nally, the 40 cm condition shifts the virtual LED beyond the board
while keeping it within the headset field of view, approximating a
detached-cue configuration.
The manipulation was restricted to horizontal translation be-
cause displacement towards the user would project the cue onto the
ground or the participant’s body, whereas displacement away from
the user could not be balanced symmetrically. Offsets outside the
field of view were excluded because they would have introduced
additional visual search and head movement costs beyond the in-
tended registration-error manipulation. The Baseline condition pro-
vided a physical reference without headset-mediated presentation.
Baseline and Control were always presented first, in fixed order,
followed by the six Offset conditions.

Each condition consisted of 30 trials. Baseline and Control re-

aligned phase (trials 1–6), a transition window (trials 7–12), and a
sustained misaligned phase (trials 13–30), with the predefined off-

Counterbalancing and RandomisationWe counterbalance
and randomise at the participant, condition, and trial levels to re-
duce anticipatory bias, learning, and order effects. At the partic-
ipant level, the order of the six Offset conditions was counterbal-
anced across participants using a 6×6 balanced Latin square. Par-
ticipants were assigned to six groups (n=4 per group), with each
group following one row of the Latin square. Offset direction (left-
ward or rightward) was randomised and balanced (left of Figure 2).
At the condition level, we randomised the onset of misalignment
within each Offset condition to capture adaptation to the sudden in-
troduction of spatial error. Specifically, the transition window (trials
7–12) contained a single randomly selected switch point, with trials
before the switch point remaining aligned and trials from the switch
point onward misaligned. The sequence of illuminated target but-
tons for each trial was also randomised within each condition to
reduce ordering effects. At the trial level, each trial used a jittered
foreperiod before stimulus onset while participants held down the
two home-position buttons on the Adaptive Controller to prevent
anticipation of LED illumination.
Pilot Study and Design RefinementA pilot study with six
volunteers was conducted to refine the final protocol. Based on the
pilot, the number of trials per condition was increased from 20 to
30 to provide a longer sustained-misalignment phase for stabilised
RT and MT estimates. Manual alignment was replaced with QR-
based registration for greater consistency across participants. The
response timeout was increased from 2s to 5s to allow sufficient
time for response selection under misalignment. Finally, offset di-
rection was balanced leftward and rightward in the final study to
avoid anticipatory bias.
4.5 Measures
We collected both objective behavioural measures and subjective
self-report measures. Behavioural measures comprised Reaction
Time (RT), Movement Time (MT), task correctness, and timeout
events recorded on each trial. All task events, including cue on-
sets and offsets, button presses, and home-position hold and release
timestamps, were logged with millisecond precision.
RT was defined as the interval from cue onset to movement
initiation as the hand off the adaptive controller. MT was de-
fined as the interval from movement initiation to the target-button
press. This separation was motivated by prior accounts of motor
behaviour suggesting that pre-movement processes, including cue
interpretation, response selection, and movement specification, can
be dissociated from execution-stage processes such as online cor-
rection [44, 19, 36, 46]. We analysed RT and MT separately in
order to distinguish delays arising primarily before movement on-
set from delays arising during target acquisition. In the present
task, RT primarily indexes cue interpretation, response selection,
and movement initiation, whereas MT primarily indexes execution
after initiation.
Subjective measures comprised a pre-study questionnaire, a
post-condition questionnaire administered after each experimental
condition, and a post-study questionnaire administered at the end of
the session. All scale-based questions used a 7-point Likert scale,
and the full questionnaires are provided in Appendix Section A.1.
The pre-study questionnaire collected demographic information
on age, English fluency, gender, prior use of VR/AR, dominant eye
and hand, current fitness, and vision/safety information.
The post-condition questionnaire has three sections. The first
section is a series of questions adapted from one-question HCI post-
experiment surveys. These questions assess perceived task diffi-
culty [45, 41], ease of use [4], motivation [37, 38], and concentra-
tion [33]. For these custom items, several scales included verbal
midpoint anchors at response option 4 following the source of the
questions. The second section is an adapted version of the Witmer
& Singer Presence Questionnaire tailored to the control panel, light
cues, and task [49]. As discussed in Section 2.5, a consensus on
how to measure presence in AR has not yet been reached so we do
not claim to be measuring it here. Instead, we are using the ques-
tions within the Witmer & Singer Presence Questionnaire to eval-
uate responsiveness, naturalness of interaction, anticipation of sys-
tem behaviour, involvement, speed of adjustment to the light cues,
perceived proficiency, visual distraction, and the ability to concen-
trate on the task rather than on the interaction mechanism. The third
section is the NASA-TLX adapted to a 7-point Likert-scale [18].

4.6 Procedure
Participants first completed the pre-study questionnaire. They then
received instructions for the task, including the visual and auditory
response feedback, and adjusted the table height so that all buttons
could be reached comfortably while standing.
Participants next completed three practice blocks. First, they
performed 12 practice trials without the headset using the physical
LEDs only. They then wore the headset and completed 12 aligned
AR practice trials, followed by 12 practice trials with a small lateral
offset. During practice, participants were also asked to deliberately
tap an incorrect button once to experience error feedback.
After practice, the experimental session began. Each participant
completed eight conditions: Baseline, Control, and the six Offset
conditions. Baseline was completed without the headset, followed
by the aligned Control condition, and then the six Offset conditions
in the pre-assigned counterbalanced order. After each condition,
participants completed the post-condition questionnaire. After all
eight conditions, they completed the post-study questionnaire.

Reaction time was not normalised by target row since the target

condition and phase.
Objective performance was analysed using trial-level mixed-
effects models. For categorical analyses of normalised RT and
MT, we used condition-phase as a fixed factor, included trial num-
ber as a covariate, and included participant as a random intercept.
Continuous analyses replaced the categorical condition coding with
registration-error magnitude. Model selection between linear and
quadratic trends was based on AIC.

recovery was analysed by aligning trials to the misalignment switch

blocks separately from Baseline and Control, which were fixed at
the start of the session.
Questionnaire data were analysed at the participant level. Non-
TLX items were tested across conditions using Friedman tests.
Bonferroni correction was applied separately within the usabil-
ity and engagement item families, and significant omnibus effects
were followed by Bonferroni-corrected Wilcoxon signed-rank tests.
NASA-TLX ratings were summarised and compared across condi-
tions in parallel with the non-TLX subjective measures.
5 RESULT
5.1 H1: Objective Performance
Increasing registration error impaired objective task performance,
supporting H1. Temporal costs increased with offset magnitude,
and correctness deteriorated once the displaced cue entered more
ambiguous spatial relationships to neighbouring targets.
As shown in Figure 3, Baseline, Control, and the aligned phases
of the six switch conditions remained close to the zero-offset refer-
ence, whereas offset-phase values increased with registration-error
magnitude. This separation was small at the smallest offset, became
clearer by the intermediate conditions, and was largest in the Large,
VLarge, and Huge conditions. At the largest offsets, offset-phase
participant means were centred at approximately 1.6×baseline for
RT and 1.4×baseline for MT.
Trial-level mixed-effects models supported this pattern. Rela-
tive to the Control offset phase, every misaligned offset increased
RT, from Small (ratio = 1.059,p< .001) to Huge (ratio = 1.605,
p< .001). MT showed the same overall pattern, although the Small
offset did not differ reliably from Control offset (ratio = 1.022,
p=.213); from Half onward, offset-phase MT increased signifi-
cantly, ranging from 1.1 times Control at Half to 1.44 times Control
at Huge. Baseline was slightly faster than Control aligned for RT
(ratio = 0.947,p=.007), whereas the corresponding MT difference
was not reliable (ratio = 0.969,p=.162).
Continuous analyses showed the same graded pattern when per-
formance was indexed by registration-error magnitude directly. For
normalized RT, a quadratic model fit better than a linear model
(∆AIC=92.21); for normalized MT, a linear model fit better than a
quadratic alternative (∆AIC=8.22, in favour of the linear model).

5.2 Adaptation After the Switch to Misalignment
Figure 6 shows switch-aligned recovery of normalised RT and MT
after the onset of misalignment. In all switch conditions, both mea-
sures increased immediately after the switch and then declined over
subsequent trials, consistent with short-term adaptation. However,
the magnitude and persistence of this disruption depended on offset
size. In the Small condition, RT and MT returned close to their orig-

costs in the Large, VLarge, and Huge conditions. A further pattern

5.3 Learning and Fatigue
Across the six randomised switch-condition blocks, we found lit-
tle evidence for a broad session-wide fatigue effect. Block-order
effects were not reliable for normalised RT or correctness, and the
only reliable block-order effect for MT was a small offset-specific
decrease across later switch blocks (β=−0.0104,p=.043). By
contrast, within-block trial progression was reliable for RT, MT, and
correctness (allp< .001), indicating local adaptation within blocks
rather than general fatigue across the session.

6 LIMITATIONS
The task provides a simple and reproducible way to characterise
the behavioural effects of static registration error. Because the
cue-response mapping was discrete and tightly controlled, the task
could capture gradual increases in both temporal cost and target
confusion as misalignment grew. However, the findings should be
interpreted within the scope of the current design. The task in-
volved rapid discrete target acquisition on a fixed physical layout,
using static offsets in a single viewing configuration. Only horizon-
tal translational offsets are tested; vertical, depth, rotational errors,
or dynamic jitter are not examined, limiting generality. Different
patterns may emerge for continuous interaction, 3D placement, ro-
tational error, dynamic jitter, denser layouts, or longer adaptation
periods. Practice before the main session may also have contributed
to performance stability in the easier conditions.
A second limitation concerns the display and rendering setup.
The study used a video see-through display rather than an opti-
cal see-through one. This helped to reduce accommodation-related
confounds and kept large offsets visible. However, it also means
that the results should not be treated as directly equivalent to opti-
cal see-through viewing. Furthermore, the virtual cue did not in-
corporate hand occlusion. We chose not to use the available SDK
hand-occlusion pipeline because its instability introduced a stronger
distraction than the absence of occlusion, but this remains a limita-
tion of the present implementation. Additionally, the head position
are not fixed so deviation of perspective might affect performance.
A third limitation concerns the subjective measures. We delib-
erately retained questionnaire-based assessment as part of the con-
tribution, but the adapted Witmer and Singer items should not be
interpreted as a validated measure of AR presence. They were
used as task-relevant ratings of interaction quality, including nat-
uralness, anticipation and adjustment speed. This framing supports
the present study; it does not establish a general psychometric in-
strument for mixed-reality presence.
Finally, the present movement analysis was designed for con-
servative interpretation within this task rather than for a full Fitts-
style account. The row-based formulation outperformed the Fitts-
style alternative on the current dataset, and the latter would in any
case require more precise information about movement start posi-
tion (we could not reliably capture which hand is used ). We there-
fore interpret the present MT analysis as a task-specific account of
movement execution under misalignment rather than as a general
statement against Fitts-style modelling.
7 DISCUSSION
7.1 Behavioural Effects of Registration Error
The zero-offset conditions (Baseline and Control) showed only a
modest difference. This suggests that, although Baseline and Con-
trol were not identical, the main degradations observed in the Offset
conditions are not just a result of the headset-based presentation.
Overall, the objective results support H1: increasing registration
error impaired performance. As the error magnitude increased, both
RT and MT rose, indicating rising costs in both response prepara-
tion and movement execution. However, the continuous models
show that this cost was not proportional to error magnitude across
the full range. Instead, the quadratic fit showed mild tapering at the
largest offsets, consistent with a ceiling-like effect in which addi-
tional displacement continued to impair performance but with di-
minishing incremental cost.
7.2 Ambiguity Rather Than Absolute Offset Alone
The correctness pattern suggests that the most sensitive region was
the ambiguity range around the Half and Medium conditions. These
offsets produced the clearest rise in wrong-button selection, which
is consistent with the cue remaining close enough to the board to

lem becomes less about immediate adjacent-button ambiguity and
more about remapping under a larger displacement. Huge intro-
duced a further change. Once the cue has become detached from

the offset and the need to interpret the cue as an external reference

biguity and displacement magnitude are partly separable factors,
even though they covaried in the present design. It also suggests
a possible interaction between them: Half and Medium may pro-
duce stronger ambiguity at lower displacement, whereas the larger
conditions produce stronger displacement with lower local ambi-
guity. The ceiling-like temporal pattern may therefore reflect the
summed effect of increasing magnitude and decreasing ambiguity
across the offset range. This possible interplay between ambiguity
and displacement magnitude is the subject of future work.
7.3 Adaptation After the Switch to Misalignment
The switch analysis suggests that participants adapted to a sta-
ble misalignment within a condition, but that this adaptation was
only partial once the offset became sufficiently large. In all switch
conditions, RT and MT increased immediately after the onset of
misalignment and then declined over subsequent trials, indicating
short-term learning of the altered cue–target relationship.
However, this recovery was offset-dependent. In the Small con-
dition, both measures returned close to their original aligned levels,
suggesting that the smallest imposed error could be accommodated
relatively quickly. By contrast, Medium and larger offsets retained
visible late-stable costs, indicating that brief exposure within a sin-
gle block was not sufficient to remove the performance penalty en-
tirely. A more specific pattern emerges when the late-stable RT
and MT values are compared directly. The gap between late-stable
RT and late-stable MT was especially pronounced in the Medium
and Large conditions. This gives a more specific interpretation of
recovery: not just incomplete adaptation, but a condition-specific
residual cost after adaptation that may lie more strongly in response
selection or cue interpretation than in movement execution. In addi-
tion, RT and MT were inconsistent in the late stable region and un-
der high workload, which could be attributed to a behavioural shift
with Large-Huge offsets, as vision dominates over proprioception.
One plausible interpretation is that this reflects the same inter-
play between ambiguity and displacement magnitude seen else-
where in the results. In the Medium and Large conditions, the cue
may remain difficult to interpret even after brief adaptation because
it is still close enough to the board to compete with nearby buttons
while no longer preserving a uniquely specified target. By contrast,
the Small condition remains sufficiently clear, while in the most ex-
treme condition, the cue may be treated more consistently as a dis-
placed external reference. Under this view, the persistent late-stable
RT cost may reflect continued ambiguity in cue interpretation rather
than movement difficulty alone.
These findings suggest that adaptation to registration error was
not uniform across the offset range. Some misalignments could be
absorbed with relatively little lasting cost, whereas others continued
to impair performance even after repeated exposure and adaptation.
This again points to the importance of the cue–target relationship,
rather than absolute offset magnitude alone, in determining how
disruptive a given registration error becomes during interaction.
7.4 Subjective Response and Questionnaire
The subjective measures complement the behavioural findings.
Consistent with H2, increasing registration error worsened per-
ceived task difficulty and workload, with the clearest increases
emerging at the larger offsets. NASA-TLX ratings showed a simi-
lar pattern, particularly for mental demand, effort, frustration, and
perceived performance cost. This indicates that the behavioural

penalties associated with misalignment were also experienced by
participants as a more effortful and less manageable interaction.
The broader questionnaires add a second layer of interpretation.
Larger offsets did not mainly reduce fun or general involvement.
Instead, the exploratory item pattern is more consistent with a loss
of interaction fluency, predictability, and perceived controllability.
Participants reported poorer naturalness, anticipation, adjustment
speed, and perceived proficiency, together with greater visual in-
terference, while responsiveness, involvement, and task focus were
less consistently affected after correction. This shows that the ex-
periential cost of registration error in this task was not primarily a
loss of engagement. Rather, it was a degradation in how directly
and confidently the virtual cue could be used to guide action.
7.5 Implications for Task-Specific Registration Require-
ments
The main design implication is that acceptable registration error is
not just determined by the geometric offset. In our task, the be-
havioural effect of misalignment depended not only on displace-
ment magnitude, but also on whether the displaced cue entered an
ambiguity-inducing relationship with nearby targets. Small offsets
produced only limited practical cost under the current study design,
whereas the clearest breakdown emerged when the cue no longer
preserved an unambiguous association with a single button. This
suggests that registration requirements should be specified relative
to both offset magnitude and local ambiguity, using explicit be-
havioural criteria such as RT, MT, error rate, and subjective burden.
At the same time, the switch analysis shows that users can adapt
to stable registration errors, at least to some extent. For this rea-
son, it would be inaccurate to claim that all AR systems must re-
tain one fixed level of tracking accuracy irrespective of task. The
present findings are better interpreted within the scope of a direct
and simple rapid target-acquisition task, in which participants re-
ceived training and responded to discrete controls on a regular lay-
out. Under other tasks or contexts, the same absolute offset might
have a different consequence. For example, an offset may remain
tolerable when the cue still uniquely specifies one target, yet be-
come much more disruptive when the same cue falls between adja-
cent controls and creates local ambiguity. Designers should there-
fore aim not only to reduce registration error in general, but also
to avoid ambiguous cue–target situations in which misalignment
weakens unique target association.
A further implication is methodological. In the presence of reg-
istration error, correctness may be an insufficient indicator of user
impact because participants may still complete the task correctly
after adapting. More sensitive measures may be low-level perfor-
mance deviations from baseline, such as RT or residual late-stable
cost after adaptation. These measures may provide a more reliable
indication that registration error is affecting the user, even when
overt task success remains high.
8 FUTUREWORK
The study can be extended in several different ways First, future
work should capture richer behavioural measures in more detail.
Eye-tracking data, such as gaze and pupil diameter, could help re-
veal attentional allocation and hesitation during cue interpretation.
Additional physiological sensing, such as thermal imaging, may
also provide complementary estimates of cognitive load [9, 29].
Hand tracking could support more precise analysis of reach trajec-
tories and online correction, similar to prior visually guided reach-
ing studies [30, 31]. Measures such as contact duration, applied
pressure, or weighted estimates of commitment may further help
interpret user intention during the task, for example, by revealing
hesitation while holding or before movement initiation.
A second priority is to dissociate ambiguity from error magni-
tude more directly. In the present design, these factors covaried
across conditions, so their individual contributions could not be es-
timated independently. Future studies should vary target spacing
and offset magnitude independently to determine whether perfor-
mance costs are driven primarily by local cue–target ambiguity, dis-
placement magnitude itself, or an interaction between the two.
Such a design would also support a clearer test of Fitts-style
movement effects under registration error. In the current setup, only
0 cm and 5 cm fell clearly within the unambiguous range, which is
insufficient to examine how movement cost scales with registration
error alone. A denser set of small, unambiguous offsets would make
this analysis more feasible. At the same time, the current task used
fixed button locations, so repeated exposure may enhance the con-
tribution of proprioception to the setup [40]. Future studies could
therefore use smaller controls, randomised target placement, or al-
ternative layouts to reduce layout-specific motor learning and better
isolate the contribution of visual guidance.
The present results also suggest that correctness alone may be
an insufficient indicator of impact on the user. Participants may
adapt sufficiently to complete the task correctly while still showing
residual deviations from baseline performance. Future work should
therefore examine whether lower-level measures, such as RT, MT,
or residual late-stable cost after adaptation, provide more sensitive
task-based indicators of user burden under misalignment.
Finally, a practical direction is ambiguity checking during the
design process. Information already available in location-based au-
thoring tools or VR-based simulation workflows could be used to
test whether expected registration errors would place a cue in an
ambiguous relationship with nearby targets before deployment. In
this way, registration quality could be evaluated not only in terms
of geometric offset, but also in terms of ambiguity risk. Reasoning-
based systems, including large language models, may also assist
by helping identify likely confusion cases from scene and layout
descriptions, although this would require separate validation.
9 CONCLUSION
This paper investigated how increasing static registration error
affects performance and subjective response in an AR target-
acquisition task. Using a controlled video see-through setup and
a reproducible 3×3 button-acquisition configuration, we anal-
ysed RT, MT, correctness, and post-condition questionnaire ratings
across multiple offset magnitudes defined relative to target spacing.
Overall, the results show that static registration error reduced
both the efficiency and the quality of AR interaction in this task.
Small offsets were relatively tolerable, whereas larger offsets led
to a clearer breakdown in speed, correctness, and perceived flu-
ency. The effect increased gradually, but it also showed a more
distinct shift once the displacement became large enough to create
target ambiguity. Participants showed short-term adaptation after
the onset of misalignment, but larger offsets retained residual per-
formance costs, particularly in late-stable RT.
Taken together, these findings suggest that registration require-
ments should be defined with respect to both error magnitude and
ambiguity risk under a specific task geometry, rather than by ab-
solute offset alone. More broadly, the study contributes a con-
trolled evaluation framework that combines behavioural measures
with questionnaire-based assessment to characterise how static reg-
istration error affects direct AR interaction.
